The [line from Andelot-en-Montagne to La Cluse](ligne:878000) crosses the Haut-Jura from north to south over a little more than 116 kilometres[^merimee]. Locally it is known as the ligne des Hirondelles, the Swallows line. It leaves the plateau at [Andelot](gare:andelot), 637 metres above sea level, climbs to 948 metres under the Savine pass, drops towards [Morez](gare:morez) and then [Saint-Claude](gare:st-claude), and ends at La Cluse, at 480 metres[^merimee]. Along the way, dozens of tunnels and viaducts had to be fitted into terrain that left almost no room for a railway.

Where the sources give different dates or figures, this article quotes both.

## A line built in six stages

A law of 11 June 1863 provided for a branch from Andelot to [Champagnole](gare:champagnole), granted to the Paris, Lyon and Mediterranean railway company (PLM)[^wikipedia]. It opened in July 1867: on the 14th according to the chronology of the Wikipedia article, on the 15th according to the bibliography it cites. Over some twenty kilometres there was a single intermediate station, Vers-en-Montagne.

The extension southwards had to wait for the Freycinet plan. The law of 17 July 1879, which set out the programme of lines to be built, included the link from Champagnole to La Cluse[^wikipedia][^merimee]. Work then went ahead from both ends.

In the south, the PLM opened La Cluse - [Oyonnax](gare:oyonnax) in May 1885, then Oyonnax - Saint-Claude on 10 July 1889. In the north, the track reached [Saint-Laurent-en-Grandvaux](gare:st-laurent-jura) on 10 July 1890, [Morbier](gare:morbier) on 15 May 1899 and Morez in 1900[^wikipedia]. For Morez, the article on the line gives 1 June and the article on the station 12 August. That left the hardest stretch, from Morez to Saint-Claude. Work began in 1905 and the section opened in August 1912[^merimee]. The heritage inventory gives 10 August; the Wikipedia article gives the 1st in one place and the 10th in another.

Forty-nine years passed between the law of 1863 and the opening of 1912. The [chronology](ligne:878000#chronicle) on the line page lists these steps one by one, with the source of each date.

## Thirty per thousand and a horseshoe under the mountain

The line is single track and has never been electrified[^wikipedia]. Its engineers worked at the limits of what a standard gauge line worked by steam would accept: curves of 180 metres radius and gradients of 30 millimetres per metre[^merimee]. On the Morez section, the cost came close to a million gold francs per kilometre[^merimee].

The counts of structures do not agree. The national heritage inventory lists 31 tunnels, from 18 to 2,080 metres long, 20 viaducts and about 360 bridges, footbridges and culverts[^merimee]. Wikipedia and the Montagnes du Jura tourist office give 36 tunnels and 18 viaducts[^montagnes]. In 2025, SNCF Réseau counted 36 and 22 in its estimate of the works needed[^franceinfo]. The gap probably comes from what each one counts: a short bridge, a tunnel in two parts, the Jura section alone or the whole line.

The longest tunnel, the Savine tunnel, is 2,080 metres long and passes under the pass, near the highest point of the line at 948 metres[^wikipedia]. The Frasses tunnel, 1,044 metres long, forms an almost closed horseshoe: the train comes out roughly below the point where it went in[^wikipedia].

Between Morbier and Morez the line loses 125 metres of height. As the crow flies, the two stations are about one and a half kilometres apart; by rail it takes nearly 5.6 kilometres of curved viaducts and tunnels[^gare-morez]. The [Crottes viaduct](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Morez_viaduc_des_Crottes01_2014-09-06.jpg), shown at the top of this article, runs above the rooftops of Morez.

## Morez, a terminus in the middle of the line

Morez has never been a through station. The line arrived there in 1900 as a terminus and was extended to Saint-Claude in 1912, leaving the station in the opposite direction. Trains still reverse there: the driver changes cab and the train sets off southwards[^wikipedia]. From 1921 to 1958, Morez was also linked to Switzerland by the metre gauge line from Nyon to Saint-Cergue and Morez[^gare-morez].

## Where the name comes from

No document from the time explains the nickname, and the accounts differ. The Montagnes du Jura tourist office reports a local explanation: the people of Morez, watching the workers on viaducts clinging to the slope, said they were brushing past the swallows[^montagnes]. Wikipedia links the name to the grace of the viaducts instead[^wikipedia]. The nickname first referred to the Franche-Comté part of the line; SNCF has since registered it as a trademark[^wikipedia].

## Railcars, pupils and tourists

The PLM steam trains gave way to railcars. Between May 2003 and December 2006, the X 2800 railcars were replaced by X 73500 railcars and X 76500 multiple units, known as AGC[^wikipedia].

The line carries the SNCF "tourist train" label, and guided trips run from February to October[^wikipedia]. It is also used by boarders at the Champagnole secondary school and by some of the roughly 800 students of the optics school in Morez, nearly 200 of them boarders from Belfort, Vesoul or Chambéry[^franceinfo].

The heritage inventory counts 20 stations, 25 level crossing keepers' houses and 2 halts when the line was built; many have been demolished since the 1960s[^merimee]. The line is recorded in the heritage inventory but has no protection as a historic monument.

## A line cut in two

The southern part of the line lost its trains in two stages. From 29 August 2005 to 13 December 2010, the section from Oyonnax to [Brion](gare:brion-montral-la-cluse) was closed while the line from Bourg-en-Bresse to Bellegarde was modernised, then reopened to regional trains from Bourg[^wikipedia]. On 10 December 2017, services stopped between Saint-Claude and Oyonnax because there was no money to renew those 31 kilometres; coaches replaced them[^wikipedia][^franceinfo].

Since then, the Hirondelles line that passengers can still ride runs from Andelot to Saint-Claude. Further south, Oyonnax is still served from Bourg-en-Bresse.

## 2025, the year it nearly stopped

The remaining part is hardly in better condition. The 2015-2020 State-Region plan contract put 6 million euros into it, then a further 1.7 million up to 2022; according to Wikipedia, 40 million were still needed after 2022[^wikipedia]. In May 2025, franceinfo reported that SNCF was asking for 90 million euros to renew the most worn track, the tunnels and the viaducts, and that the line was at risk of suspension by 2026. The Bourgogne-Franche-Comté region, which alone pays for the upkeep of the line, then asked the State for help. On 31 May 2025, the national federation of transport users' associations (Fnaut) demonstrated for keeping the line[^franceinfo].

The Region pays alone because of a trade-off: since January 2024, the line from Nevers to Chagny has been part of the so-called structuring network, whose renewal SNCF Réseau pays for in full. In return, the Region took on all investment on Andelot - Saint-Claude, according to the specialist blog Quai n°2. The same blog wrote in October 2025 that the line nearly stopped with the 2026 timetable: against a theoretical need of 85 million euros, the Region finally released 12 million, enough to keep it going for three years with four return trips a day[^quai2]. Its president summed up the effort as avoiding the worst without guaranteeing the best[^quai2].

As of 22 November 2026, we have found no official announcement that settles the future of the line beyond those three years. The VFF line page will be updated when there is one.
